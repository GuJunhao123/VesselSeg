Abstract.Accurate segmentation of hepatic and portal veins is critical
for planning liver resections and guiding surgical interventions. Stan-
dard voxel-wise methods often struggle with extreme variations in ves-
sel scale and complex topologies, which frequently leads to fragmented
branches and ambiguous bifurcations. We address these issues through
a hierarchical framework that treats vascular segmentation as a task
of structured anatomical decomposition. By integrating geometric reg-
ularization with hierarchical modeling, our approach captures vascular
organization and leverages anatomical priors inherent in hepatic struc-
tures. Specifically, the Adaptive Anatomical Hierarchy Decomposition
(AAHD) module partitions the vessel tree into trunks, small branches,
and junctions to guide feature learning based on anatomical structure.
In parallel, the Topology-Guided Feature Refinement (TGFR) module
incorporates junction-specific information into the main segmentation
stream to improve connectivity at bifurcations. A Hierarchical Anatomi-
cal Consistency Loss (HACL) is further employed to enforce spatial rela-
tionships between trunk, branch, and junction components while main-
taining structural consistency within each vascular system. Experiments
on public benchmarks indicate that our framework outperforms current
state-of-the-art methods, particularly when segmenting sub-millimeter
branches and maintaining bifurcation integrity. Beyond standard overlap
metrics, our method demonstrates superior topological robustness and
cross-dataset generalization. This backbone-agnostic paradigm enhances
structural coherence in existing architectures with negligible parameter
overhead.

1 Introduction
Accurate segmentation of hepatic vessels from enhanced computed tomography
(CT) is essential for assessing tumor resectability, performing living-donor liver
transplantation, and planning anatomic resections [20,3,18]. The liver consists
of two anatomically distinct vascular systems, namely the hepatic veins (HV)
and portal veins (PV), which must be precisely separated to define Couinaud
functional segments and safe resection margins [7,11]. However, achieving such

precision remains a significant challenge due to the intricate morphological char-
acteristics of the liver’s circulatory network [12].
This difficulty stems from the fact that hepatic vasculature is organized
hierarchically with significant scale variations, ranging from major trunks to
sub-millimeter branches [21]. This complexity, combined with sharp geometric
changes at bifurcations and low contrast against the liver parenchyma, makes
accurate prediction difficult. Effective models must maintain connectivity within
each vessel tree while ensuring anatomical exclusivity between different systems
[15]. Although deep networks achieve high voxel-wise overlap, they often fail to
capture hierarchical anatomical roles because these properties are typically only
weakly constrained during training.
Traditional techniques like vesselness filtering [4], active contours, and center-
line tracking [13] are noise-sensitive and frequently miss distal branches. While
deep learning models have evolved from U-Net [19] to self-configuring frame-
works like nnU-Net [8], and more recent works have explored topology-aware
objectives [27,22,14], state-of-the-art models still struggle to preserve structural
integrity. In particular, peripheral discontinuities and scale ambiguity persist
because current methods rarely integrate hierarchical anatomical roles directly
into feature refinement [10,17] or structural regularization [1,26].
Considering the hierarchical topology of hepatic vessels, an anatomically con-
sistent hierarchical decomposition strategy is proposed for structural vessel seg-
mentation. Specifically, we partition vessels into trunk, branch, and junction
components via adaptive thresholding for supervision by anatomical role. Fea-
tures are refined through asymmetric gating driven by topology, while structural
separation is enforced by a hierarchical consistency loss incorporating orthogo-
nality and exclusion penalties. By embedding anatomical priors as differentiable
constraints, this paradigm effectively mitigates scale-related artifacts and en-
hances the robustness of bifurcations. The key contributions are summarized as
follows:
1. Based on vascular branching priors, our hierarchical factorization into trunk,
branch, and junction roles prevents optimization issues caused by scale dif-
ferences and provides a geometric guide to resolve topological gaps;
2. Topology-guided refinement is achieved through junction-sensitive gating,
while a hierarchical anatomical consistency loss enforces residual relation-
ships and inter-role constraints;
3. Extensive experiments on public benchmarks show that our method im-
proves the preservation of fine branches and generalizes well across different
datasets.

2.2 Topology-Guided Feature Refinement
To address fragmented bifurcations, the TGFR module refines backbone de-
coder features using localized topological cues. LetF pri denote the backbone
decoder output. An auxiliary decoder processes stopped-gradient primary fea-
turessg[F pri]to produce auxiliary representationF aux and junction prediction
Jvia a dedicated junction head. These generate topology gateGand modulation
featureF m:
G= sg[σ(J)],F m = sg[ψ dil(Faux)],

whereσand⊙denote the sigmoid function and element-wise multiplication,

centrates feature refinement on anatomically complex bifurcations where con-
nectivity is most prone to fragmentation. The stop-gradient operator decouples
the auxiliary branch from the backbone and prevents refinement-induced gradi-
ents from flowing into the auxiliary pathway, allowing topological cues to act as
a forward-only structural prior for stabilizing bifurcation regions.

3 Experiments
3.1 Datasets and Evaluation Metrics
The framework is evaluated on two public contrast-enhanced 3D CT datasets
featuring expert hepatic and portal vein annotations. Primary training and val-
idation are conducted on the Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD Task 08)
dataset [2], using the re-annotated version provided by Xu et al. [26]. This ver-
sion focuses on a subset of 61 thin-slice cases (axial thickness≤2.0 mm) with
refined hepatic and portal system masks, which were partitioned into training
and validation sets at an 8:2 ratio. Furthermore, cross-dataset robustness is eval-
uated using the 3D-IRCADb dataset [23] as an external test set, which consists
of 20 cases acquired across varying imaging protocols and contrast phases to test
generalization capabilities.
Performance is measured via Dice Similarity Coefficient, 95% Hausdorff Dis-
tance (HD95), sensitivity, and precision, following standard practices [26].
3.2 Implementation Details
All experiments were implemented in PyTorch on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs.
Prior to training, the CT volumes were resampled to an isotropic resolution of
1.0 mm, clipped to a window of[0,400]HU, and z-score normalized. The network
was subsequently trained on96×96×96patches extracted via label-weighted
cropping. Furthermore, data augmentations including random flipping, axial ro-
tations, and Gaussian noise were employed to improve model generalization. Op-
timization was performed using AdamW for 1,000 epochs, with an initial learning
rate of1×10 −3 and a cosine annealing schedule to ensure stable convergence. To
ensure structural stability and anatomical coherence, optimal hyperparameters
were determined through a coarse to fine grid search within the range[0.01,0.5]
on the validation set, resulting in loss weights ofλt = 0.2, λ s = 0.1, λ j = 0.05
and auxiliary coefficients ofα=β= 0.05, γ= 0.02.

4 Conclusion
We presented a hierarchical framework for liver vessel segmentation that uses
anatomical decomposition and geometric regularization to handle varying vessel
scales and fragile topologies. Our experiments show that this approach improves
both segmentation accuracy and structural integrity across different network
architectures. Although the current method relies on anatomically derived role
supervision, future research could investigate end-to-end adaptive role learning
to improve performance in the presence of complex pathological changes or vary-
ing imaging conditions.
